Question 1 of 10 · Fill-in-the-blank THIS WEEK
The physiotherapist performed __________ to restore gliding mobility to the median nerve following carpal tunnel decompression surgery.
A) nerve conduction testing
B) neuromobilisation
C) neurostimulation
D) neuroprolotherapy
Show Answer
B) neuromobilisation
Neuromobilisation (also called neural mobilisation or neurodynamic techniques) involves gentle movement exercises that restore the normal sliding and tensioning mechanics of peripheral nerves within their surrounding tissues. It is commonly used post-operatively to reduce neural adhesions and improve nerve excursion.
Question 2 of 10 · Definition match THIS WEEK
Which term describes an unpleasant, abnormal sensation — spontaneous or evoked — often characterised by a burning, electric, or crawling quality, distinct from normal pain?
A) paraesthesia
B) hyperalgesia
C) dysaesthesia
D) allodynia
Show Answer
C) dysaesthesia
Dysaesthesia refers to an unpleasant abnormal sensation, which may be spontaneous or stimulus-evoked. It differs from paraesthesia (pins and needles — not necessarily unpleasant) and allodynia (pain from a normally non-painful stimulus). It is commonly reported in polyneuropathy and central sensitisation.
Question 3 of 10 · Fill-in-the-blank THIS WEEK
Following total hip arthroplasty, the patient was commenced on low molecular weight heparin as __________ to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis during the early post-operative period.
A) anticoagulation maintenance
B) thromboprophylaxis
C) haemostasis therapy
D) fibrinolytic treatment
Show Answer
B) thromboprophylaxis
Thromboprophylaxis refers to preventive measures taken to reduce the risk of thrombus formation, particularly venous thromboembolism (VTE). In surgical patients, this typically involves pharmacological agents (e.g. LMWH, aspirin) and/or mechanical measures (e.g. compression stockings, early mobilisation).
Question 4 of 10 · Fill-in-the-blank THIS WEEK
The patient demonstrated impaired __________, reporting significant difficulty sensing the movement of her lower limbs when her eyes were closed during balance assessment.
A) proprioception
B) nociception
C) kinaesthesia
D) stereognosis
Show Answer
C) kinaesthesia
Kinaesthesia is the sense of movement of body parts, derived from muscle spindle and joint receptor afferents. It differs from proprioception (static joint position sense) by specifically referring to the dynamic perception of motion. Impaired kinaesthesia is common in peripheral neuropathy, joint injury, and ageing.
Question 5 of 10 · Fill-in-the-blank THIS WEEK
The treating physiotherapist applied __________ to deliver a corticosteroid preparation transdermally to the inflamed supraspinatus tendon, utilising ultrasound as the delivery mechanism.
A) iontophoresis
B) phonophoresis
C) electrophoresis
D) shortwave diathermy
Show Answer
B) phonophoresis
Phonophoresis (also called sonophoresis) uses therapeutic ultrasound to drive topical medications — typically anti-inflammatory agents such as corticosteroids — through the skin into underlying tissues. This distinguishes it from iontophoresis, which uses electrical current for the same purpose.
Question 6 of 10 · Collocation complete THIS WEEK
The patient presented with lateral hip pain that was reproduced on palpation over the greater trochanter, consistent with a diagnosis of __________ bursitis.
A) iliotibial
B) ischial
C) trochanteric
D) acetabular
Show Answer
C) trochanteric
Trochanteric bursitis refers to inflammation of the bursa overlying the greater trochanter of the femur. It presents with lateral hip pain reproduced on direct palpation and may be related to iliotibial band tightness, hip abductor tendinopathy, or gluteal muscle imbalance. Note: current nomenclature often uses "greater trochanteric pain syndrome" (GTPS).
Question 7 of 10 · Fill-in-the-blank PREVIOUS
On auscultation of the lung bases, fine __________ were noted bilaterally at end-inspiration, raising clinical suspicion of pulmonary oedema in this post-cardiac-surgery patient.
A) wheeze
B) crepitations
C) friction rub
D) stridor
Show Answer
B) crepitations
Crepitations (also called crackles) are discontinuous, non-musical adventitious breath sounds heard on auscultation, typically at end-inspiration. Fine crepitations at the lung bases are characteristic of pulmonary oedema or early interstitial fibrosis, whereas coarser crepitations may indicate secretions in the airways.
Question 8 of 10 · Definition match PREVIOUS
Which term refers to the protrusion of an organ or tissue through an abnormal opening in the wall that normally contains it, most commonly applied to intervertebral disc pathology in physiotherapy?
A) calcification
B) herniation
C) fibrosis
D) stenosis
Show Answer
B) herniation
Herniation describes the protrusion of tissue beyond its normal anatomical boundary. In the spine, disc herniation occurs when the nucleus pulposus displaces through a tear in the annulus fibrosus, potentially compressing adjacent nerve roots or the spinal cord, producing radicular pain and neurological deficit.
Question 9 of 10 · Definition match PREVIOUS
Which term describes the surgical removal of all or part of the fibrocartilaginous structure within the knee joint that functions in load distribution, shock absorption, and joint stability?
A) synovectomy
B) chondroplasty
C) meniscectomy
D) arthrodesis
Show Answer
C) meniscectomy
Meniscectomy is the surgical removal of the meniscus (partial or total). The menisci are C-shaped fibrocartilage structures that distribute load across the tibiofemoral joint. Total meniscectomy significantly increases contact stress on articular cartilage and is associated with accelerated osteoarthritis — hence the preference for partial or meniscus-preserving surgery where possible.
Question 10 of 10 · Collocation complete PREVIOUS
The athlete sustained a complete rupture of the anterior __________ ligament during a non-contact deceleration manoeuvre, confirmed on MRI and requiring surgical reconstruction.
A) collateral
B) cruciate
C) annular
D) coracoacromial
Show Answer
B) cruciate
The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is an intra-articular, extrasynovial ligament of the knee that primarily resists anterior tibial translation and rotational forces. ACL rupture is common in sports requiring pivoting and deceleration. The "cruciate" name derives from the Latin crux (cross), as the ACL and PCL cross each other within the joint.

1 clinical passage (approx. 220 words) + 5 comprehension questions

Clinical Article Excerpt: Rehabilitation Following Osteochondral Injury of the Knee The management of osteochondral injuries presents a significant clinical challenge in sports physiotherapy. These injuries involve damage to both the articular cartilage and the underlying subchondral bone, and are frequently encountered following traumatic events, including hamstring avulsions or torsional loading of the knee. Following surgical intervention or conservative management, patients typically undergo a prescribed period of immobilisation to facilitate tissue healing. During this phase, clinicians must monitor carefully for the onset of dysaesthesia, which may signal peripheral nerve compromise arising from post-operative oedema or sustained positional loading. As progressive weight-bearing is reintroduced, intra-articular effusion and pain levels must be systematically tracked to guide programme advancement. Restoration of neuromuscular control — including proprioception and kinaesthesia — is considered essential prior to return-to-sport clearance. Current evidence indicates that premature return to high-impact activities following osteochondral repair substantially elevates the risk of re-injury and accelerated joint degeneration. Physiotherapists therefore play a pivotal role in educating patients regarding realistic recovery timelines and ensuring adherence to a structured, evidence-based rehabilitation programme throughout the continuum of care.

Reading Question 5 of 5 · Vocabulary in context
In the passage, the phrase "intra-articular effusion" most closely refers to:
A) Swelling of the surrounding periarticular tendons
B) Fluid accumulation within the joint cavity itself
C) Calcification deposits within the joint space
D) Subchondral bone marrow oedema
Show Answer
B) Fluid accumulation within the joint cavity itself

Together, "intra-articular effusion" describes a joint effusion — excess fluid within the synovial joint space, commonly following trauma or inflammation.

Writing Self-Assessment Checklist — 10 Criteria

  • Correct letter format: date, recipient address, salutation, body paragraphs, closing, sender details
  • Opening paragraph clearly identifies the patient (name, age, diagnosis) and states the purpose of the referral
  • Relevant surgical and medical history summarised concisely (discectomy, polyneuropathy, T2DM)
  • Post-operative course conveyed accurately, including immobilisation and thromboprophylaxis details
  • Current neurological presentation described (dysaesthesia, kinaesthesia deficit, hamstring tightness)
  • Treatment to date is mentioned (neuromobilisation commenced by inpatient team)
  • Clear and specific referral request made (specialist neurological physiotherapy — not generic)
  • Patient goals referenced to demonstrate purposeful, patient-centred referral
  • British English spelling used throughout (e.g. immobilisation, oedema, anaesthesia)
  • Appropriate register: formal, professional, clinical — no colloquial language; sentence length controlled (no run-ons)

Role Play 1 — Outpatient Physiotherapy Clinic

Setting: You are a physiotherapist seeing Mr Daniel Thompson (38M, office worker) for his first appointment. He was recently diagnosed with early-stage parkinsonism and referred by his neurologist. He is anxious and mentions that his voice has become noticeably quieter, affecting his ability to communicate at work.

Your roles: Physiotherapist (you) + Examiner playing Mr Thompson

Key task: Explain the role of physiotherapy in managing parkinsonism, address his concern about his voice (dysphonia), and outline the importance of assessing his kinaesthesia and movement quality.

Suggested opening: "Good morning, Mr Thompson. I'm Leo, your physiotherapist. I understand this has been a worrying time since your diagnosis — thank you for coming in today. How are you feeling?"

"Physiotherapy plays an important role in maintaining mobility and balance in parkinsonism..." "Regarding your voice, you may find it helpful to see a speech therapist alongside our sessions — that's a common concern with parkinsonism." "I'd like to assess your sense of movement, called kinaesthesia, as this is often affected early on." "Are there specific activities at work or at home that have become more difficult for you?" "Our aim is to help you maintain your quality of life and stay as active as possible."
Show Model Answer Outline
Opening (rapport): Acknowledge diagnosis, normalise anxiety, invite patient to share concerns.

Explain physiotherapy role: PT for parkinsonism focuses on gait retraining, balance, flexibility, fall prevention, and maintaining neuromuscular function. Use of cueing strategies (auditory, visual, tactile).

Address dysphonia: Acknowledge concern empathically. Explain that dysphonia (reduced vocal volume and quality) is common and is primarily managed by speech-language therapy. Offer to co-ordinate referral. Mention that breathing exercises and postural work done in physiotherapy can complement voice therapy.

Kinaesthesia assessment: Explain that you will assess his kinaesthesia (sense of movement) as part of the neurological screening — this helps identify how well proprioceptive and sensorimotor systems are functioning.

Closing: Summarise plan, invite questions, provide reassurance that early intervention yields better outcomes.

Role Play 2 — Hospital Ward, Post-Surgical

Setting: You are the ward physiotherapist at Auckland City Hospital. Mrs Aroha Patel (55F) underwent right knee arthrodesis (knee fusion) two days ago for end-stage osteoarthritis. She is uncertain about what movement is allowed, and is worried about "blood clots." She will be discharged in 48 hours.

Your roles: Physiotherapist (you) + Examiner playing Mrs Patel

Key task: Educate Mrs Patel on her immobilisation requirements and weight-bearing restrictions, explain thromboprophylaxis measures in place, and teach her a simple home exercise programme appropriate for her stage of recovery.

Suggested opening: "Good afternoon, Mrs Patel. I'm Leo from the physiotherapy team. I've come to help you understand what to expect as you recover from your knee operation, and to do some exercises with you before you go home. Is this a good time?"

"Following arthrodesis, your knee will remain in a fixed position permanently — I want to explain what that means for your day-to-day life." "Your team has started you on injections to prevent blood clots — this is called thromboprophylaxis. It's routine after this type of surgery." "During the immobilisation period, it's important not to put full weight through the leg until the surgeon gives the all-clear." "These ankle pumping exercises will help keep blood moving and reduce swelling in your foot." "Do you have someone at home who can help you manage stairs and getting in and out of the bathroom?"
Show Model Answer Outline
Opening (rapport + consent): Greet warmly, confirm timing is appropriate, establish rapport.

Explain arthrodesis outcome: The knee joint has been surgically fused — this eliminates pain but permanently prevents knee bending. Normalise this outcome; focus on functional gains (pain relief, stability).

Immobilisation and weight-bearing: Explain non-weight-bearing or toe-touch weight-bearing (per surgeon order). Describe use of crutches/frame. Clarify immobilisation period (typically 6–12 weeks with cast or brace).

Thromboprophylaxis education: Address her blood clot concern empathically. Explain that the medical team has started enoxaparin (anticlotting injection) — this is thromboprophylaxis. Teach her to recognise DVT signs (calf swelling, redness, warmth) and when to seek help.

Home exercise programme: Ankle pumps (10x hourly), static quadriceps contractions, deep breathing exercises, elevation of limb. Written instructions offered.

Discharge planning: Assess home environment, stairs, equipment needs. Confirm follow-up plan.
