Q1 · Fill-in-the-Blank herniation · Jul 22
"MRI of the lumbar spine confirmed a significant disc _______ at the L4–L5 level, with the extruded nuclear material compressing the left L5 nerve root."
A) calcification
B) herniation
C) stenosis
D) fusion
Show Answer
B) herniation — A disc herniation occurs when the inner nucleus pulposus protrudes through a tear in the outer annulus fibrosus, potentially compressing adjacent nerve roots. "Calcification" refers to calcium deposits; "stenosis" is narrowing of a canal; "fusion" is surgical joining of vertebrae.
Q2 · Definition Match prehabilitation · Jul 22
Which term describes: "A structured exercise and conditioning programme undertaken by a patient before a planned surgical procedure, with the aim of improving functional reserve and optimising postoperative recovery outcomes"?
A) rehabilitation
B) mobilisation
C) prehabilitation
D) reconditioning
Show Answer
C) prehabilitation — "Prehabilitation" (or "prehab") specifically denotes pre-operative preparation through exercise. "Rehabilitation" occurs post-injury or post-surgery. "Mobilisation" refers to movement techniques. "Reconditioning" describes restoring fitness without the pre-operative connotation.
Q3 · Fill-in-the-Blank crepitations · Jul 25
"On auscultation of the lung bases, the physiotherapist noted bilateral fine _______, which, combined with the patient's elevated respiratory rate, raised concern for developing pulmonary oedema."
A) stridor
B) wheezes
C) crepitations
D) rhonchi
Show Answer
C) crepitations — Fine bilateral basal crepitations (also called crackles) heard on auscultation are a hallmark sign of pulmonary oedema, resulting from fluid in the alveoli. Stridor is a high-pitched inspiratory sound from upper airway obstruction. Wheezes indicate bronchospasm. Rhonchi arise from secretions in large airways.
Q4 · Definition Match discectomy · Jul 26
Which term describes: "A surgical procedure involving the partial or complete removal of an intervertebral disc, most commonly performed to relieve pressure on a nerve root or the spinal cord"?
A) laminectomy
B) arthrodesis
C) foraminotomy
D) discectomy
Show Answer
D) discectomy — A discectomy removes disc material compressing neural structures. A laminectomy removes part of the vertebral lamina to widen the spinal canal. Arthrodesis is the surgical fusion of a joint. Foraminotomy widens the intervertebral foramen to decompress a nerve root.
Q5 · Fill-in-the-Blank neuromobilisation · Jul 26
"Following lumbar surgery, the physiotherapist initiated _______ techniques — including slider and tensioner neural mobilisations — to restore nerve root mobility and reduce ongoing radicular symptoms."
A) neuromobilisation
B) traction
C) electrotherapy
D) manipulation
Show Answer
A) neuromobilisation — Neuromobilisation (also known as neural mobilisation or neurodynamic therapy) uses specific limb movements to mobilise the nervous system within its connective tissue sheath. "Traction" applies a distraction force to the spine. "Manipulation" is a high-velocity technique applied to joints. "Electrotherapy" encompasses modalities such as TENS or ultrasound.
Q6 · Fill-in-the-Blank asterixis · Jul 24
"During the neurological examination, the clinician observed _______ — a brief, involuntary flapping tremor of the outstretched hands upon wrist extension — which may indicate hepatic or metabolic encephalopathy."
A) chorea
B) athetosis
C) asterixis
D) dystonia
Show Answer
C) asterixis — Asterixis (sometimes called a "liver flap") is characterised by brief lapses in sustained posture, producing a flapping tremor. It is associated with hepatic encephalopathy, uraemia, and hypercapnia. Chorea is irregular, dance-like involuntary movements. Athetosis involves slow, writhing movements. Dystonia is a sustained muscle contraction causing abnormal postures.
Q7 · Fill-in-the-Blank synovectomy · Jul 17
"After two years of persistent knee synovitis that was unresponsive to conservative physiotherapy and corticosteroid injections, the orthopaedic surgeon recommended a _______ to reduce joint inflammation."
A) meniscectomy
B) osteotomy
C) synovectomy
D) arthroscopy
Show Answer
C) synovectomy — A synovectomy is the surgical excision of the inflamed synovial membrane, performed when synovitis is refractory to conservative management. A meniscectomy removes part of the meniscus. An osteotomy cuts and realigns bone. An arthroscopy is a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure using a camera inside the joint — it is a method of access, not the specific procedure described.
Q8 · Definition Match fibrosis · Jul 18
Which term describes: "The pathological process of excess fibrous connective tissue forming in an organ or tissue as a reparative response to injury or inflammation, often resulting in functional impairment"?
A) necrosis
B) fibrosis
C) oedema
D) hypertrophy
Show Answer
B) fibrosis — Fibrosis is an excess accumulation of extracellular matrix components (mainly collagen), leading to scarring and stiffness. Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis are well-known examples. Necrosis is cell death. Oedema is excess interstitial fluid. Hypertrophy is an increase in cell size and organ volume.
Q9 · Collocation Complete meniscectomy · Jul 19
"Following an arthroscopic _______, the patient was commenced on a graduated weight-bearing programme and range-of-motion exercises within 24 hours of the procedure."
A) meniscectomy
B) appendectomy
C) lobectomy
D) tonsillectomy
Show Answer
A) meniscectomy — An arthroscopic meniscectomy is the surgical removal of all or part of a torn meniscus in the knee. It is commonly followed by early weight-bearing and physiotherapy to restore function. The other options are procedures on the appendix, lung lobe, and tonsils respectively — unrelated to knee rehabilitation.
Q10 · Collocation Complete oximetry · Jul 16
"Continuous pulse _______ was maintained throughout the patient's cardiorespiratory physiotherapy session to ensure that peripheral oxygen saturation remained above 92%."
A) manometry
B) spirometry
C) tonometry
D) oximetry
Show Answer
D) oximetry — Pulse oximetry (SpO₂) non-invasively measures peripheral oxygen saturation via a sensor applied to the finger or earlobe. Manometry measures pressure (e.g. oesophageal). Spirometry measures lung volumes and flow rates. Tonometry measures intraocular pressure.

Clinical Article Excerpt — Journal of Orthopaedic Physiotherapy, Vol. 14

Pre-operative prehabilitation programmes have gained considerable traction in the management of patients awaiting lumbar disc surgery. Historically, clinicians delayed physiotherapy input until the postoperative period; however, emerging evidence suggests that targeted exercise prior to surgery may substantially improve recovery trajectories. A recent retrospective audit conducted at a tertiary spinal unit reviewed outcomes for 78 patients with confirmed disc herniation at the L4–L5 or L5–S1 level who underwent elective microdiscectomy. Preoperative imaging in 31% of cases additionally revealed peripheral calcification within the annulus fibrosus, a finding associated with longer symptom duration. All participants demonstrated pre-operative lumbar hypomobility on standardised segmental assessment, with mean lumbar flexion reduced to 42% of predicted normal values. Postoperatively, a structured physiotherapy protocol was initiated within 48 hours of surgery. This included progressive neuromuscular strengthening, patient education, and — crucially — neuromobilisation techniques targeting residual neural adhesions and mechanosensitivity along the sciatic pathway. Patients who had completed a formal prehabilitation programme achieved independent ambulation on average 1.4 days earlier than those who had not. Additionally, 14 patients presented with concurrent trochanteric bursitis, complicating their rehabilitation by limiting single-leg stance and stair negotiation. A modified treatment plan, incorporating hip abductor strengthening and offloading strategies, was adopted for this subgroup. Overall patient satisfaction at 12-week follow-up was reported as high in 84% of participants.

Reading Q5 · Vocabulary in Context
In the context of the passage, the term "hypomobility" most closely means:
A) Complete absence of movement in the lumbar spine
B) Excessive, unstable movement of the lumbar vertebrae

Role Play 1 — Post-Discectomy Home Exercise Programme

Target words: discectomy neuromobilisation hypomobility

Setting: Surgical orthopaedics ward, day before discharge. You are the physiotherapist; the patient (interlocutor) is Alan Morrison, 55-year-old male, day 4 post lumbar microdiscectomy.

Key Tasks:

  • Explain the purpose of the home exercise programme (HEP) and its importance after a discectomy.
  • Describe two to three specific exercises, including neuromobilisation (nerve gliding) exercises to address hypomobility and residual radicular symptoms.
  • Address the patient's concern: "Will the exercises be painful? I'm worried about making things worse."
  • Explain what movements or activities to avoid and when to seek medical advice.

Suggested Opening: "Mr Morrison, now that you're getting ready to go home tomorrow, I'd like to go through some exercises that will really help your recovery after the discectomy. Is that all right?"

"After a discectomy, it's important to…" "These nerve gliding exercises — we call them neuromobilisation — help to…" "Because your back has been quite stiff — what we call hypomobility — these gentle movements will…" "You should stop and contact us if you notice…" "That's a very understandable concern — let me reassure you that…"
Show Model Answer Outline
Model Answer Outline:
  1. Opening: Acknowledge the patient's progress; explain the HEP is the next step in recovery from the discectomy.
  2. Exercise 1 — Walking: Begin with 10-minute walks three times daily, increasing duration gradually. Explain why: promotes circulation, prevents scar tissue adhesion, addresses post-operative hypomobility.
  3. Exercise 2 — Neuromobilisation (sciatic nerve slider): Sitting, gently extend knee while flexing foot, then return. Explain it helps restore nerve mobility after the surgery. Emphasise "no sharp, shooting pain" — mild tension is acceptable.
  4. Exercise 3 — Pelvic tilts / gentle core activation: Lying, flatten lumbar spine to bed; builds protective stability without loading the disc.
  5. Addressing concerns: Validate — "It's very natural to feel anxious." Distinguish between "therapeutic discomfort" (acceptable) and "pain worse than before surgery or new neurological symptoms" (stop and call). Use the phrase "listen to your body."
  6. Restrictions: Avoid lifting more than 5 kg, prolonged sitting >30 min, bending and twisting simultaneously. Red flags: increased leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, loss of bladder/bowel control — seek emergency help immediately.
  7. Closing: Confirm follow-up with community physiotherapy; invite questions; provide written HEP sheet.

Role Play 2 — Managing Breathlessness in a Patient with Valvular Heart Disease

Target words: crepitations diastolic valvular

Setting: Outpatient cardiorespiratory physiotherapy clinic. You are the physiotherapist; the patient (interlocutor) is Mrs Sandra Osei, 68-year-old female, referred for cardiac rehabilitation following an acute exacerbation of heart failure secondary to valvular heart disease.

Key Tasks:

  • Explain what valvular heart disease is and why it caused her breathlessness, in simple terms.
  • Explain what crepitations are and why the medical team were listening to her lungs.
  • Introduce the concept of diastolic dysfunction in lay language if it comes up.
  • Address the patient's question: "My doctor mentioned my diastolic something — what does that mean?"
  • Discuss the goals of her physiotherapy programme: pacing, breathlessness management, and graduated exercise.

Suggested Opening: "Good morning, Mrs Osei. I'm Leo, one of the physiotherapists here. I understand you've had a difficult few weeks with your breathing — I'd like to talk through what's been happening and how we can work together to help. Is that OK?"

"Your heart has a valve — think of it like a door — that isn't closing properly…" "The crackling sounds the doctors heard in your lungs — called crepitations — are caused by…" "Diastolic refers to when the heart relaxes and fills with blood — when that's affected…" "We call this activity pacing — the idea is to…" "If you notice your breathing becomes much worse during exercise, please stop and…"
Show Model Answer Outline
Model Answer Outline:
  1. Opening: Empathetic acknowledgement of the patient's experience; establish rapport before clinical discussion.
  2. Explaining valvular heart disease: Use the "door" analogy — "One of the valves in your heart, which acts like a door controlling blood flow, isn't functioning properly. This means blood can back up, and fluid can build up in your lungs." Avoid excessive medical jargon.
  3. Explaining crepitations: "The crackling sounds the doctor heard — called crepitations — are the sound of small amounts of fluid in the tiny air sacs of your lungs. That's why your breathing feels harder. The treatment you've had is helping drain that fluid."
  4. Explaining diastolic dysfunction (if raised): "Diastolic refers to the relaxation phase of your heartbeat — when your heart fills with blood. If the heart muscle is stiff, it doesn't fill as well, and that can make the whole system work harder. Your cardiologist is monitoring this closely."
  5. Physiotherapy goals: (a) Breathlessness management — diaphragmatic breathing, pursed-lip breathing for symptom control; (b) Activity pacing — break tasks into stages, rest before becoming breathless; (c) Graduated exercise — start with short, flat-surface walking and increase slowly using a perceived exertion scale (Borg RPE); (d) Monitoring — teach the patient to recognise worsening symptoms (increased ankle swelling, orthopnoea, sudden breathlessness) and when to seek urgent help.
  6. Closing: Summarise the plan; invite questions; provide written information on the cardiac rehab programme.
