Question 1 · Fill in the Blank THIS WEEK · immobilisation
The orthopaedic team advised complete ___ of the wrist for six weeks following the distal radius fracture, using a below-elbow plaster cast.
A) mobilisation
B) immobilisation
C) manipulation
D) traction
Show Answer
B) immobilisation — Immobilisation refers to preventing movement of a body part to promote healing, typically following fractures or ligament injuries. Mobilisation (A) is the opposite. Manipulation (C) refers to passive joint movement. Traction (D) involves applying a pulling force.
Question 2 · Fill in the Blank THIS WEEK · metatarsalgia
The 42-year-old marathon runner presented with diffuse forefoot pain aggravated by prolonged running, consistent with a diagnosis of ___ secondary to overuse and inappropriate footwear.
A) plantar fasciitis
B) Morton's neuroma
C) metatarsalgia
D) hallux valgus
Show Answer
C) metatarsalgia — Metatarsalgia is a broad term for pain and inflammation in the metatarsal region of the foot (the ball of the foot). Plantar fasciitis (A) affects the heel and plantar arch. Morton's neuroma (B) causes neuralgia between toes. Hallux valgus (D) is a bony deformity of the great toe.
Question 3 · Fill in the Blank THIS WEEK · myocardial
The 12-lead electrocardiogram revealed ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF, confirming an acute inferior ___ infarction requiring emergency percutaneous coronary intervention.
A) hepatic
B) renal
C) myocardial
D) pulmonary
Show Answer
C) myocardial — Myocardial refers to the muscular tissue of the heart (myocardium). Myocardial infarction (MI) is the clinical term for a heart attack. The prefix myo- means muscle; cardial relates to the heart.
Question 4 · Fill in the Blank PREVIOUS · hamstring
The 26-year-old sprinter was referred to physiotherapy following a grade II ___ strain sustained at the proximal musculotendinous junction, presenting with localised posterior thigh tenderness and reduced knee flexion strength.
A) quadriceps
B) hamstring
C) gastrocnemius
D) adductor
Show Answer
B) hamstring — The hamstrings (biceps femoris, semimembranosus, semitendinosus) run along the posterior thigh. They are the most commonly strained muscle group in sprinting athletes. The clue "posterior thigh" and "knee flexion" localises the injury to the hamstring group.
Question 5 · Fill in the Blank PREVIOUS · osteochondral
MRI imaging of the left ankle identified a grade III ___ lesion on the medial talar dome, accounting for the patient's persistent joint pain and mechanical clicking following a previous inversion sprain.
A) intraosseous
B) periosteal
C) osteochondral
D) cortical
Show Answer
C) osteochondral — An osteochondral lesion involves both bone (osteo-) and cartilage (chondral). These lesions of the talus frequently result from ankle sprains. Intraosseous (A) refers to within the bone itself. Periosteal (B) relates to the bone's outer membrane. Cortical (D) refers to the dense outer layer of bone.
Question 6 · Definition Match THIS WEEK · echolalia
Which term describes the involuntary, automatic repetition of words or phrases spoken by another person, commonly observed in patients with autism spectrum disorder, Tourette syndrome, or aphasia?
A) dysphasia
B) echolalia
C) apraxia
D) dysarthria
Show Answer
B) echolalia — From the Greek echo (repetition) and lalia (speech). Dysphasia (A) is impaired language comprehension or expression. Apraxia (C) is the inability to perform purposeful movements despite intact motor function. Dysarthria (D) is slurred or unclear speech due to motor dysfunction.
Question 7 · Definition Match PREVIOUS · dysaesthesia
Which term describes an unpleasant abnormal sensation — either spontaneous or evoked — often reported by patients as burning, tingling, or an electric-shock quality, associated with peripheral neuropathy or spinal cord pathology?
A) paraesthesia
B) hyperalgesia
C) dysaesthesia
D) allodynia
Show Answer
C) dysaesthesia — Dysaesthesia (dys- = abnormal; aesthesia = sensation) is specifically unpleasant and often burning. Paraesthesia (A) includes both pleasant and unpleasant abnormal sensations (e.g. "pins and needles"). Hyperalgesia (B) is an exaggerated response to painful stimuli. Allodynia (D) is pain from stimuli that are not normally painful (e.g. light touch).
Question 8 · Definition Match THIS WEEK · acromioclavicular
Which term refers to the joint formed between the acromion process of the scapula and the lateral end of the clavicle, commonly injured in contact sports and falls onto an outstretched hand?
A) glenohumeral
B) sternoclavicular
C) coracoclavicular
D) acromioclavicular
Show Answer
D) acromioclavicular — The acromioclavicular (AC) joint is a synovial joint at the top of the shoulder. AC joint sprains are graded I–VI using the Rockwood classification. The glenohumeral (A) joint is the main ball-and-socket shoulder joint. The sternoclavicular (B) joint is at the medial clavicle. Coracoclavicular (C) refers to the ligaments connecting the coracoid process to the clavicle.
Question 9 · Collocation Complete THIS WEEK · iliotibial
Complete the clinical collocation: "iliotibial ___ syndrome" — a common overuse condition causing lateral knee pain in runners and cyclists.
A) tract
B) band
C) ligament
D) tendon
Show Answer
B) band — "Iliotibial band syndrome" (ITBS) is the correct clinical term. The iliotibial band (ITB) is a thick band of fascia running along the lateral thigh from the iliac crest to the tibia. Although "tract" (A) is anatomically used (tractus iliotibialis), in clinical practice the term "band" is standard when describing the syndrome.
Question 10 · Collocation Complete PREVIOUS · strabismus
Complete the clinical collocation: "convergent ___" — a condition in which one or both eyes turn inward, also known as esotropia, commonly detected in paediatric ophthalmological assessments.
A) strabismus
B) nystagmus
C) ptosis
D) diplopia
Show Answer
A) strabismus — Convergent strabismus (esotropia) is one of two main forms; the other is divergent strabismus (exotropia). Nystagmus (B) is involuntary rhythmic eye movement. Ptosis (C) is drooping of the eyelid. Diplopia (D) is double vision, which may be a symptom of strabismus.

Clinical Case Summary — Acute Coronary Syndrome

Mr. David Pearce, a 58-year-old non-smoking accountant with a background of hypertension and hypercholesterolaemia, presented to the emergency department following a 90-minute episode of central chest pain radiating to his left arm. He reported a two-day prodromal period of increasing fatigue and mild jaw discomfort, which he had attributed to stress. His wife noted that similar episodes had occurred during physical activity over the preceding three weeks, suggesting a pattern of exertional angina that had been escalating in frequency.


On initial assessment, an electrocardiogram demonstrated ST-segment elevation in the anterior leads (V1–V4), prompting activation of the hospital's primary percutaneous coronary intervention pathway. Troponin I levels were markedly elevated at 4.2 ng/mL, confirming acute myocardial infarction. Echocardiography revealed anterior wall hypokinesis with an ejection fraction of 38%, consistent with significant left ventricular dysfunction.


Emergency coronary angiography identified a 95% occlusion of the left anterior descending artery. Successful angioplasty with drug-eluting stent insertion restored normal coronary blood flow (TIMI grade 3). Post-procedurally, the patient was commenced on dual antiplatelet therapy and a high-intensity statin. A pulmonary embolism was excluded via CT pulmonary angiography. Cardiac rehabilitation was recommended as part of his discharge planning, with physiotherapy involvement to supervise graded exercise reconditioning.

Question 1 · Factual
According to the case summary, what diagnostic test confirmed the acute myocardial infarction in addition to elevated troponin levels?
A) Echocardiography showing reduced ejection fraction
B) CT pulmonary angiography
C) An electrocardiogram demonstrating ST-segment elevation
D) Coronary angiography identifying arterial occlusion
Show Answer
C) — The passage states: "an electrocardiogram demonstrated ST-segment elevation in the anterior leads (V1–V4), prompting activation of the primary PCI pathway." The ECG plus elevated troponin confirmed the diagnosis. Echocardiography (A) revealed ventricular dysfunction but was not mentioned as a confirmatory test for MI.
Question 2 · Factual
What intervention was performed to restore coronary blood flow in Mr. Pearce?
A) Thrombolytic therapy with alteplase
B) Emergency coronary artery bypass grafting
C) Angioplasty with drug-eluting stent insertion
D) Cardioversion under general anaesthesia
Show Answer
C) — The passage explicitly states "Successful angioplasty with drug-eluting stent insertion restored normal coronary blood flow (TIMI grade 3)." Angioplasty involves inflating a balloon catheter to open a blocked artery, often followed by stent placement.
Question 3 · Inferential
What can be inferred from the description of Mr. Pearce's "prodromal period" of fatigue and jaw discomfort in the two days before presentation?
A) These symptoms were unrelated to the myocardial infarction
B) The patient had a prior diagnosis of temporomandibular disorder
C) Early warning symptoms of myocardial ischaemia can present atypically before a full infarction
D) Jaw pain is pathognomonic for posterior myocardial infarction
Show Answer
C) — The word "prodromal" indicates symptoms that precede a full-blown disease episode. This passage illustrates that cardiac ischaemia may first present with atypical symptoms (fatigue, jaw pain) before a definitive MI. This is a key clinical education point: prodromal symptoms are early indicators. Option D is incorrect as jaw pain is not pathognomonic (uniquely specific) to posterior MI.
Question 4 · Inferential
What does the description of "exertional angina escalating in frequency" suggest about the clinical course of Mr. Pearce's condition prior to admission?
A) The patient had stable angina that was well-controlled with medication
B) His coronary artery disease was likely progressing, indicating unstable angina
C) Exertional angina is a normal finding in men over 50
D) The symptoms were primarily due to deconditioning rather than coronary artery disease
Show Answer
B) — Angina that increases in frequency and occurs at lower thresholds of exertion is characteristic of unstable angina (a type of acute coronary syndrome), indicating progressive coronary artery disease. This escalating pattern is clinically significant and distinguishes unstable from stable angina, which would be predictable and consistent in its triggers.
Question 5 · Vocabulary in Context
In the final paragraph, a "pulmonary embolism was excluded." In this context, what does "pulmonary" most precisely refer to?
A) Relating to the muscular walls of the heart
B) Relating to the lungs and their blood supply
C) Relating to the systemic arterial circulation
D) Relating to the pericardium
Show Answer
B) — "Pulmonary" derives from the Latin pulmo (lung). A pulmonary embolism is a blood clot in the pulmonary arteries (the arteries supplying the lungs). CT pulmonary angiography is the gold-standard imaging to rule it out. Pulmonary embolism was excluded because its symptoms (chest pain, dyspnoea) can mimic acute MI.

Role Play 1 — Neurological Rehabilitation Setting

Setting: Inpatient neurological rehabilitation unit, Auckland City Hospital

Your role: Physiotherapist

Patient role: Mrs. Yuki Tanaka, 62-year-old woman, three weeks post-left hemisphere stroke. Her adult daughter is accompanying her.

Key task: Explain Mrs. Tanaka's communication difficulties (echolalia, reduced psychosocial engagement) to her daughter, and outline the multidisciplinary rehabilitation plan and realistic goals. Address the daughter's anxiety about her mother's recovery.

Vocabulary to use: echolalia psychosocial contraindication

Suggested opening: "Mrs. Tanaka's daughter, thank you for joining us today. I wanted to take some time to explain what we've been observing and what our rehabilitation team is planning going forward…"

"one of the speech patterns we're seeing is called echolalia, which means…" "the psychosocial aspects of recovery are just as important as the physical…" "there are no contraindications to commencing rehabilitation at this stage…" "our multidisciplinary team, which includes speech therapy, occupational therapy, and physiotherapy…" "recovery from stroke is gradual — small improvements week by week are meaningful…"
Show Model Answer Outline
Opening: Introduce yourself and acknowledge the daughter's concern. Establish rapport and check understanding.

Explanation of echolalia: Define in plain language — "Your mother sometimes repeats back words or phrases that we say to her. This is called echolalia and is a recognised feature of certain types of aphasia after stroke. It does not mean she cannot understand, and it often improves with speech therapy."

Psychosocial dimension: "Stroke recovery is not only physical. The psychosocial impact — that is, how the experience affects your mother emotionally, her sense of self, and her social connections — is something we address with the whole team, including psychology and social work."

Rehabilitation plan: Outline physiotherapy goals (mobility, transfers, safe ambulation) and confirm no contraindications to active rehab at this stage.

Closing: Invite questions, provide written information, confirm next family meeting date.

Role Play 2 — Outpatient Musculoskeletal Setting

Setting: Outpatient physiotherapy clinic

Your role: Physiotherapist

Patient role: Mr. James Kovacs, 29-year-old competitive cyclist, six weeks post-acromioclavicular joint injury. He is keen to return to racing and is frustrated by his slow progress.

Key task: Explain the current rehabilitation stage, clarify when it will be safe to return to competitive cycling, advise on the importance of progressive weight-bearing through the upper limb, and address his impatience regarding the timeline.

Vocabulary to use: acromioclavicular immobilisation weight-bearing

Suggested opening: "Mr. Kovacs, thanks for coming in today. I can see from my notes that you've been making good progress since your injury six weeks ago. Let's talk about where things are at and what the next steps look like…"

"the acromioclavicular joint has gone through an important healing phase over the last six weeks…" "now that the immobilisation phase is complete, we can begin loading the joint more progressively…" "weight-bearing through the arm — such as in a press-up or handlebar position — needs to be reintroduced gradually…" "returning to racing before the joint is fully rehabilitated puts you at risk of re-injury…" "I'd expect you to be back on the bike within four to six weeks, provided we follow the programme…"
Show Model Answer Outline
Opening: Acknowledge the patient's frustration empathetically. Validate his goals (return to racing) while setting realistic expectations.

Current status: "The initial immobilisation phase is complete and the acromioclavicular joint has healed to the point where we can begin the strengthening and functional restoration phase."

Weight-bearing progression: Explain that the cycling position requires upper limb weight-bearing through the handlebars, and this must be reintroduced gradually to avoid re-injury. Start with closed-chain exercises before returning to the bike.

Timeline: Provide an evidence-based return-to-sport timeline. Explain that competitive racing (high vibration, potential falls) requires full functional restoration — typically 10–12 weeks post-injury for grade II AC joint sprains.

Closing: Give a home exercise programme, book review in two weeks, encourage questions. Use motivational interviewing to maintain engagement.
